## 5. Strategy and Business Themes

**Anti-drone systems**
- Zen claims a differentiated, fully indigenous, IP-owned wideband anti-drone system that was the only one to qualify in 2023 and covers frequencies beyond commercial bands. It is offered as an integrated soft-kill plus hard-kill solution with subsidiaries. **[CHECK: NUMBER MISMATCH: 2023]**
  > we were the only people who qualified in that.
- The anti-drone pipeline has become very strong after Operation Sindoor and is expected to lead demand.
  > the pipeline for the anti-drone systems has become very strong post-Operation Sindoor
- Threats evolve (ground-controlled to GPS-guided to AI waypoint drones), so systems need an upgrade every two to three years, creating continuing demand. The roadmap targets detection from 100 MHz to 12 GHz. **[CHECK: NUMBER MISMATCH: 100, 12]**
  > the systems will be required every two to three years upgradation will be required
- Soft-kill jamming has negligible cost per engagement. Zen also integrates hard kill with L70 and other weapons, which management says is not very expensive.
  > as long as it is not a hard kill, it is just a soft kill blocking, jamming the signal
- Zen does not make drones. It is focused on anti-drone systems, and Vector Techniques is working on a better counter to Shahed-type drones.
  > we are not doing drones at this point in time, we are focusing exceptionally on anti-drone systems
**Simulators**
- Simulators have a typical 10-year lifecycle, creating a recycling or refurbishment market. Demand is driven by nations preparing for war and needing faster, continuous training.
  > they will have a typical lifecycle of 10 years.
- Zen is building its own air force simulators and is also looking at acquisitions in this area. Simulators will be offered across three services, domestic and international.
  > we are trying to build our own air force simulators, but we are also looking at some interesting acquisitions in that regard.
**Exports**
- Export focus regions are Africa, the Middle East and CIS. Southeast Asia is being accessed via ARI's Singapore office. Both simulators and anti-drone systems are in demand, with many anti-drone inquiries.
  > Africa is one region that we really focus on, we are looking at Middle East, we are looking at CIS countries
**Subsidiaries and M&A**
- Acquisitions (ARI for naval simulation, AI Turing for remote control weapon stations, Vector Techniques for drones, Bhairav Robotics for robotics) are strategic extensions. Their incremental solutions are included in the Rs.6000 Crores target. **[CHECK: NUMBER MISMATCH: 6000]**
  > the incremental solutions being offered by these companies are included in that, subsumed in that.
- No pivot beyond simulation and anti-drone is planned unless there is an exceptional acquisition. The aim is global number one or two in three to five years, with a market of a couple of billion dollars in each segment.
  > we will go deeper into what we have because these markets have a lot of potential.
**R&D and new products**
- The edge is attributed to deep in-house R&D and IP ownership. Zen is working on futuristic technologies and would partner only on a genuine technology collaboration, not as a manufacturing partner for foreign technology.
  > we are open to technical partnership as long as it is a technological partnership
**AI**
- AI is used both in internal processes and in products. Examples are an AI coach in simulators giving one-on-one training and threat classification in anti-drone systems. Management said it is at a very basic level of integration.
  > AI has become a real obsession within the company.
**Competition and moat**
- Management says the government's push for indigenous IP, with software and design visibility, favours Zen over importers and resellers. It concedes that armed forces decentralisation means rivals may pick up some orders.
  > there may be some orders that the others are picking up.
- Zen has no direct government nomination as PSUs like BEL do, even though IDDM gives single-vendor benefits. Management argues BEL gets other orders, not anti-drone ones.
  > under IDDM, we get single vendor benefits but most of the time we will never be nominated
- Competitors are described as cobbling together vendors with stacked margins, so they would struggle to match Zen on cost. Zen is also the only company that has integrated with and demonstrated on L70 air defence guns.
  > we know one very large company that has got about six vendors together to offer an offering that we were given.

## 6. Supply Chain and Risks

- Production is largely outsourced through a value chain, and management sees no execution constraint on capacity.
  > we have enough bandwidth within the supply chain that to execute the order. So we do not think there is any constraint there.
- Customer concentration and procurement dependence on the Indian armed forces. Zen is a prime contractor, and the government is pushing it to sell to friendly nations, which management says diversifies customers.
  > Indian government is pushing us to go and sell to friendly allied nations.
- Government policy favours indigenous IP, with a demand for source-code visibility and in-house designs because of malware concerns. This favours Zen and disadvantages foreign-tech reliant competitors.
  > they are insisting that the software be ours and we should be able to read it
- PSUs like BEL can get direct nominated orders, which Zen cannot. This is a structural disadvantage in winning large deployments.
  > one of the benefits of being, public sector is that you can place you get direct orders from the government.
- Emergency procurement is time-bound (conclusion by March 2026 unless extended) and capped per order, creating timing risk and limits on ticket size.
  > they have to be concluded before March of 2026, unless there is extension given to the EP.
- Geopolitical demand drivers (multiple ongoing conflicts) support demand. Evolving threats such as AI-vision drones and high-altitude motherships require continuous R&D.
  > preparation for war is no longer an option, but an absolute necessity for every nation at this point in time.

## 7. Analyst's Read

**Positives**
- Operational EBITDA margin was resilient at 34% standalone and 38% consolidated even with a sharp revenue fall, indicating pricing power or a low fixed-cost base.
- The balance sheet is very strong: net cash of Rs.1103 Crores, no debt, asset-light, which gives room for M&A and R&D while orders are slow.
- Management guided that anti-drone tenders and RFPs are now live, with emergency procurement to conclude by March 2026, giving a visible catalyst window.
- Management said the Rs.6000 Crores cumulative target is intact and does not slip to FY29, which shows conviction (though conditional).
- Subsidiaries now hold Rs.190 Crores of the order book and drove most Q2 order wins (per analyst), showing early diversification beyond standalone Zen.
- Hard-kill capability gives Zen an integrated soft-kill plus hard-kill offering, which management says is differentiated on IP and wideband coverage.

**Concerns**
- The order book of about Rs.675 Crores is small against the Rs.6000 Crores target and H1 revenue of Rs.331.79 Crores consolidated. About Rs.300 Crores of it is AMC, so equipment visibility is thin.
- Heavy timing risk: management's own order timing was wrong (expected by September 30th). The H2 simulator order timing is still uncertain, and FY26 H2 is guided as muted.
- Reaching Rs.6000 Crores cumulatively by FY28 needs a step-change in quarterly execution (Rs.500-750 Crores per quarter versus Rs.250-300 Crores historical), and management conceded Rs.750 Crores would require changes.
- Earnings quality: the 52% total EBITDA margin and 37% PAT margin are inflated by other income (about Rs.22.7 Crores in Q2 by calculation). Core operational profit fell about 47% standalone.
- Competition in anti-drone is increasing and the mix is shifting towards it (from 40-60 to 50-50), so segment margins may converge or compress, which management brushed aside.
- Zen lacks nomination or direct-order access, unlike PSUs, and tends to win only via single-vendor or competitive routes. Emergency procurement orders are capped at under Rs.300 Crores each.
- Order wins in Q2 were small (about Rs.94 Crores per analyst) and mostly subsidiary-led, meaning the standalone business, where margins are visible, did not add much.
